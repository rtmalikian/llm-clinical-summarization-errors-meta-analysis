Clinically Significant Errors in Large Language Model-Generated Clinical Summaries: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Omissions, Inaccuracies and Fabrications per Summary

Raphael Malikian, independent researcher, Palmdale, CA, USA. ORCID: 0009-0008-4326-4205

Download the full paper (PDF)

What this review did

Large language models now write clinical summaries: discharge summaries, emergency department encounter summaries, hospital course narratives, referral letters and clinic visit summaries. A clinician reads and signs each one, and an error in it can travel with the patient. This systematic review searched PubMed, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar and ClinicalTrials.gov, and pooled, across 11 studies, the proportion of summaries that contained at least one error, using the individual summary as the denominator throughout. Screening, extraction and risk of bias assessment were each done twice, independently, with every disagreement settled by reading the source.

The headline finding is a gap in the literature

The primary outcome, the proportion of summaries with at least one clinically significant error, could not be pooled. Only 1 of the 11 included studies reported it: a prospective pilot in which 13 of 62 summaries (21.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 11.7 to 33.2) carried a critical, treatment-impacting error.

Of the 48 reports assessed in full, 31 (65 percent) reported no extractable per-summary error count at all. They reported mean errors per summary, rating scores, similarity metrics or error-instance counts instead. The paper classifies all 31 in a reporting-gap table.

What could be pooled

  • Summaries with at least one error of any audited type (supplementary pool, specified post hoc): 60.6 percent (95 percent CI 29.8 to 84.7; 5 units, 4 studies, 323 summaries).
  • Summaries with at least one omission: 20.7 percent (11.5 to 34.6; 16 units, 1011 summaries). Excluding one study that counted only a single safety fact: 40.6 percent (22.0 to 62.4).
  • Summaries with at least one fabrication or addition: 17.6 percent (2.9 to 60.0; 8 units, 1226 summaries). The paper warns that this pool is close to uninterpretable as a single frequency, because the studies counted fabrication in very different ways.
  • Summaries with at least one inaccuracy: 27.9 percent (14.6 to 46.7; 5 units, 500 summaries).
  • Potential harm of at least moderate level: one study rated 1 of 100 summaries (1.0 percent) as likely to cause moderate harm.
  • No study reported matched model and human error counts, so the model versus human comparison could not be fitted.

Certainty of evidence (GRADE) was very low for every rated outcome. Nine of 11 studies were at high risk of bias on an adapted QUADAS-2 tool, 2 were at some concerns, and none was at low risk.
