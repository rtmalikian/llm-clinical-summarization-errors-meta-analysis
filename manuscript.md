# Clinically Significant Errors in Large Language Model-Generated Clinical Summaries: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Omissions, Inaccuracies and Fabrications per Summary

Raphael Malikian

ORCID: 0009-0008-4326-4205

Affiliation: Independent researcher, Palmdale, CA, USA

Corresponding author: Raphael Malikian, rtmalikian@gmail.com

Date: 31 October 2026

This is a Cochrane-level systematic review. It is not a Cochrane review and it has not been submitted to Cochrane.

## Abstract

**Background.** Large language models (LLMs) now draft clinical summaries inside electronic health record workflows: discharge summaries, hospital-course narratives, emergency department encounter summaries, progress-note summaries, referral letters and clinic-visit summaries. A clinician reads and signs each summary, and an error in it can travel with the patient. Single studies report error counts in very different ways, and no review has pooled, across summary types, the proportion of summaries that contain at least one clinically significant error, using the individual summary as the denominator throughout.

**Objectives.** To estimate the proportion of LLM-generated clinical summaries that contain at least one clinically significant error (study-defined), and the proportions with at least one omission, at least one fabrication or addition, and at least one inaccuracy, together with errors per summary, the proportion rated as carrying potential harm of at least moderate level, and any matched comparison with human-written summaries.

**Search methods.** We searched PubMed (378 records), Europe PMC (467), preprints through Europe PMC (140), Semantic Scholar (5) and ClinicalTrials.gov (1) on 31 October 2026, for studies published from 2022 to 2026, and we chased the reference lists of the included studies. OpenAlex was blocked by a provider rate limit and was not searched. Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, CENTRAL, IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library were not run because no access route was available. This review has no PROSPERO registration number. A PROSPERO-ready form was prepared on 31 October 2026, before screening closed.

**Selection criteria.** Empirical evaluations in which an LLM generated a clinical summary from existing electronic health record source data (real or synthetic) and the study reported the number of summaries assessed and the number with errors, or data from which both could be derived. Patient-facing simplification or translation, radiology or pathology report generation from images, ambient scribe encounter notes, reviews and frameworks with no primary error counts were excluded.

**Data collection and analysis.** Screening, extraction and risk of bias assessment were each done twice, independently, by an LLM team working under one accountable human author, with every disagreement settled by reading the source. Title and abstract screening agreement gave a Cohen kappa of 0.62 for three categories and 0.67 for the binary pass or exclude split. Risk of bias used an adapted QUADAS-2 tool for LLM clinical summarisation evaluations (adapted from QUADAS-2 with QUADAS-AI signalling questions), named as adapted at every mention. Proportions were pooled with a random-effects generalised linear mixed model with a logit link, fitted in Python (scipy) with adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature because R was not available in the build environment. Certainty was rated with GRADE.

**Main results.** The search found 991 records. After 508 duplicates were removed, 483 records were screened, 48 reports were assessed in full, and 10 studies were included. Citation chasing added 1 study, giving 11 studies and 22 extraction arms (21 LLM arms and 1 human arm). The primary outcome could not be pooled: only 1 of the 11 studies reported the number of summaries with at least one clinically significant error, a prospective pilot in which 13 of 62 summaries (21.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 11.7 to 33.2) carried a critical, treatment-impacting error, and even that count is ambiguous in the source text. A supplementary pool of summaries with at least one error of any audited type, specified post hoc in Amendment 01 and labelled as such throughout, gave 60.6 percent (95 percent CI 29.8 to 84.7; 5 units, 4 studies, 323 summaries; prediction interval 6.9 to 97.0). Omissions were pooled at 20.7 percent (95 percent CI 11.5 to 34.6; 16 units, 7 studies, 1011 summaries), rising to 40.6 percent (95 percent CI 22.0 to 62.4) when a study that counted omission of a single risk fact was removed. Fabrication or addition was pooled at 17.6 percent (95 percent CI 2.9 to 60.0; 8 units, 6 studies, 1226 summaries), a figure dominated by one outlying unit and close to uninterpretable as a single frequency. Inaccuracy was pooled at 27.9 percent (95 percent CI 14.6 to 46.7; 5 units, 3 studies, 500 summaries). One study rated 1 of 100 summaries as likely to cause moderate harm (1.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 0.03 to 5.4). Errors per summary could not be pooled, and no study reported matched LLM and human error counts, so the LLM versus human contrast could not be fitted. Heterogeneity was very high in every pool (I squared above 93 percent). Nine of 11 studies were at high overall risk of bias on the adapted tool, 2 were at some concerns and none was at low risk. Certainty was very low for every rateable outcome, and the LLM versus human contrast was not rateable because there was no evidence.

**Authors' conclusions.** Errors are common in LLM-generated clinical summaries in the studies that counted them per summary, and the graded, clinically significant end of the error spectrum is the part the literature reports least. The finding of this review is as much about reporting as about frequency: a field that rarely counts clinically significant errors per summary cannot yet say how often its summaries are wrong in a way that matters. Studies should report, for every summary type and model, the number of summaries assessed, the number with at least one error of each type, and the number with at least one error graded clinically significant, so that the next review can pool the primary outcome this one could not.

## Plain language summary

**The problem.** Computer programs called large language models can write clinical summaries, the short documents that describe a hospital stay, an emergency visit or a clinic appointment. Clinicians use these summaries to hand care from one person to another. If a summary leaves something out, changes a fact, or adds something that never happened, the next clinician may act on wrong information. We wanted to know how often that happens, counting one summary at a time.

**What we did.** We searched medical databases for studies from 2022 to 2026 in which a large language model wrote clinical summaries and researchers checked each summary against the patient record and counted the mistakes. We found 11 studies. Every study was checked twice, disagreements were settled by reading the papers again, and we combined the counts with statistical models that allow the true rate to differ between studies.

**What we found.** Most studies counted any mistake, but only one study counted the mistakes it classed as serious, meaning errors it judged could change treatment. In that study, 13 of 62 summaries (21.0 percent) had such an error. Across the studies that counted mistakes of any kind, about 3 in 5 summaries had at least one. About 1 in 5 summaries missed something from the record, about 1 in 4 contained an inaccurate statement, and counts of invented content ranged from none at all to nearly every summary, depending on how the study counted. One study rated 1 summary in 100 as likely to cause moderate harm. No study counted errors in model-written and human-written summaries of the same records in the same way, so we cannot say which is safer.

**What this means.** These systems write summaries with errors often enough that each summary needs a careful check against the record before it is used. The studies disagree with each other a great deal, and our confidence in every combined figure is very low. Until studies count serious errors per summary as a routine step, nobody can give a firm overall rate.

## 1. Background

Clinical summaries carry care across boundaries. A discharge summary tells the next team what happened in hospital, what changed, and what needs to happen next. A hospital-course narrative, an emergency department encounter summary, a referral letter and a clinic-visit summary do the same work at other boundaries. Each one is a compression of a longer record, and compression is where information can be lost, changed or invented.

Large language models are now used to draft these documents from the record itself. The draft reaches a clinician who reads it, edits it and signs it. That workflow has a clear attraction: drafting takes time, clinician time is short, and a draft that is mostly right may still save work. It also has a clear risk. A summary that reads smoothly can carry an error that a reader does not spot, because the text gives no sign of where it departs from the record. Error in a generated summary is therefore a different problem from error in a human draft written slowly. The reader's check is the last line of defence, and the check is only as good as the errors are visible.

Three error types run through this review, and they do different things to care. An omission removes information the source record contained: a diagnosis, a medication change, a follow-up plan. An inaccuracy changes information: a wrong dose, a wrong date, a finding attributed to the wrong side or the wrong patient encounter. A fabrication or addition inserts information the source record does not contain: a symptom never recorded, a test never done, a history never taken. Studies label these types with their own words, including hallucination and confabulation for fabrication, and content gaps for omission. The labels differ. The underlying events are what this review counts.

Frequency and severity are separate questions, and the literature answers them unevenly. A study can count every departure from the source, down to minor wording slips, or it can count only departures that could change management. The first count is easier to produce and is the one most studies report. The second count is the one a clinician or a service lead wants when deciding how much checking a generated summary needs. This review set its primary outcome at the second count: the proportion of summaries containing at least one error the study itself graded as clinically significant. The results section shows what happened to that choice. The literature, as it stands, almost never reports it.

Related syntheses leave this question open. A PROSPERO registration, CRD420261349028 (Kharat and Agrawal, registered 23 March 2026, planned end 31 October 2026), covers hospital discharge summaries only and explicitly excludes progress notes, referral letters and operative notes. Its scope is the hospital-course narrative and the discharge summary. This review covers all clinical summary types, with discharge as a pre-specified subgroup, one primary outcome, and the individual summary as the denominator throughout. A re-check on 31 October 2026 found that record still listed as planned or ongoing, and found no Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews review or protocol on this question. Three widely cited primary studies show why per-summary counting matters and why it is rare: the discharge summary study by Williams and colleagues (JAMA Internal Medicine 2025) reported mean errors per summary rather than the proportion of summaries with at least one error; Van Veen and colleagues (Nature Medicine 2024) compared generated and expert summaries on rating scales; and Asgari and colleagues (npj Digital Medicine 2025) set out a framework for assessing clinical safety and hallucination rates. Each is discussed in the results, because each was excluded at full text for want of an extractable per-summary numerator and denominator, and that pattern is part of what this review found.

## 2. Objectives

The primary objective was to estimate the proportion of LLM-generated clinical summaries that contain at least one clinically significant error, where clinical significance is defined by the contributing study. Secondary objectives were to estimate the proportions of summaries with at least one omission, at least one fabrication or addition, and at least one inaccuracy; the mean number of errors per summary; the proportion of summaries rated as carrying potential harm of at least moderate level; and, where a study reported both, the contrast between LLM-generated and human-written summaries.

## 3. Methods

This review follows the structure of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.5, and is reported against PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-S. It is a Cochrane-style review. It is not a Cochrane review. The protocol (concept-and-protocol.md) was written on 31 October 2026, before screening began. One amendment was logged before any analysis was run (Section 3.3). The review team was an LLM team working in place of a human research team, with one accountable human author. Roles, agreement statistics and the settlement process are reported in Sections 3.6 and 3.7 and in Appendix B, and the arrangement is disclosed in the declarations, in line with the RAISE 2025 guidance on responsible AI use in evidence synthesis.

### 3.1. Criteria for considering studies for this review

**Types of studies.** Empirical evaluations of any design that reported the number of summaries assessed and the number with errors, or data from which both could be derived, published from 2022 to 2026. Peer-reviewed papers and preprints were eligible; preprints were flagged and removed in a sensitivity analysis. Reviews, editorials and frameworks with no primary error counts were excluded.

**Types of summaries and data.** The unit of analysis was an individual clinical summary generated from existing electronic health record source data, real or synthetic. Eligible summary types were hospital discharge summaries and hospital-course narratives, emergency department encounter summaries, progress-note summaries, referral letters and clinic-visit summaries, in any specialty, setting or country.

**Exposure.** The summary was generated by a large language model of any family, including fine-tuned, retrieval-augmented and agentic pipelines. Model and version were extracted where reported.

**Comparators.** The source record or structured ground truth, a clinician-authored summary, or blinded expert rating, where present. Single-arm proportions were the primary analysis, so a comparator was not required for inclusion.

**Exclusions.** Patient-facing simplification or translation of existing documents; radiology or pathology report generation from images; ambient scribe encounter notes generated from conversation; citation or reference generation; and studies with no extractable numerator and denominator per summary.

### 3.2. Outcomes

The **primary outcome**, fixed in the protocol before screening, was the proportion of LLM-generated summaries containing at least one clinically significant error, with clinical significance defined by the contributing study and the study severity or harm grade extracted alongside the count.

Secondary outcomes, in protocol order, were: the proportion of summaries with at least one omission; the proportion with at least one fabrication or addition; the proportion with at least one inaccuracy; errors per summary; the proportion of summaries rated as carrying potential harm of at least moderate level; and the LLM versus human error contrast where both were reported for the same source records under the same outcome definition.

A pool needed at least two units under the analysis plan. A single-unit outcome is reported as that unit alone, with an exact confidence interval and no pooled estimate.

### 3.3. Protocol amendments

**Amendment 01, dated 31 October 2026, logged after extraction settlement and before any analysis was run.** Settlement showed that the primary outcome was reported by exactly one arm in the included set (R0301, prospective pilot, 13 of 62), and that one further study (R0391) defined a major threshold but reported major counts at error level only. The primary outcome therefore could not be pooled under the analysis plan. Amendment 01 kept the primary outcome in its place with its not pooled verdict, and authorised one supplementary pool: the proportion of summaries with at least one error of any audited type. That pool is labelled supplementary and post hoc at every appearance in this manuscript, in the abstract, in the summary of findings table and in the conclusions. It does not replace the primary outcome. Sensitivities for the supplementary pool were set in the amendment as S1 (units from studies with any explicit severity or harm grading), S2 (excluding preprints) and S3 (excluding high risk of bias).

No other amendment was made. No extraction form change resulted from the two-pass comparison, and no separate extraction pilot phase was run; the two independent passes served as the check. That gap against the ideal team shape is recorded here rather than left silent.

### 3.4. Search methods

The search was run on 31 October 2026. The concept combined three blocks: the model (large language model, LLM, ChatGPT, GPT-4, generative AI, foundation model), the task (summarization, discharge summary, hospital course, encounter summary, progress note summary, referral letter, clinical summary) and the outcome (error, omission, hallucination, fabrication, inaccuracy, accuracy, completeness, safety), limited to 2022 to 2026. Appendix A gives the string as run for each source.

Sources searched, with records retrieved: PubMed, 378; Europe PMC (title and abstract strategy), 467; preprints through Europe PMC, 140; Semantic Scholar, 5, reached through its bulk search endpoint after the graph API returned rate limit errors on three attempts; ClinicalTrials.gov, 1. The combined total was 991 records.

Two retrieval notes belong in the open. First, a first Europe PMC run on default fields returned 6,569 hits dominated by incidental full-text mentions. That run was superseded by the title and abstract strategy before screening, and its raw output is retained in the search log. Second, OpenAlex was blocked: four search strings returned HTTP 429 with a response stating that the shared daily budget for the network address was exhausted. Under the provider rate limit rule in force for this series, that was treated as a hard stop and no retry was made in this run. OpenAlex is recorded as not searched, and the block is carried into the limitations.

Sources not run: Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, CENTRAL, IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library were not searched on 31 October 2026 because no access route was available. Ready-to-run strings for each are filed in the search log. Google Scholar was not run; it has no full programmatic interface. None of these sources is described as searched anywhere in this review.

Citation chasing: backward citation chasing was carried out on the reference lists of the 10 studies included from database searching. It produced 15 candidate records verified as absent from the 483-record search set. Candidates were screened in duplicate. Fourteen were assessed, 1 was not retrieved, 13 were excluded and 1 was included (Song 2025). No separate forward citation-chasing pass was performed. The search itself covered 2022 to 2026 across the sources above, and the missing forward pass is stated in the limitations.

Registration position: this review has no PROSPERO registration number, and none is claimed or implied. A PROSPERO-ready registration form for this question was prepared on 31 October 2026, before screening closed (stage1-search/prospero-form.md). The related registration CRD420261349028 covers discharge summaries only, as described in the Background. A registry re-check on 31 October 2026 found no new registration on this review question and no Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews review or protocol on LLM clinical summarization. The re-check used direct retrieval of the known record and web-index searches; PROSPERO own search interface could not be enumerated through the text routes available, and that limit is recorded in the re-check file.

### 3.5. Selection of studies

Deduplication was by DOI first, then PubMed identifier, then exact normalised title, with a second pass merging records whose normalised titles matched across keys. Of 991 records, 508 duplicates were removed, leaving 483 records for screening.

Title and abstract screening was dual and independent. Screener A used an inclusion-first checklist and Screener B an exclusion-first decision tree, each specified in writing before screening (stage2-screening/screening-rules-A.md and screening-rules-B.md). Screener A passed 58 records (11 include, 47 maybe) and excluded 425. Screener B passed 100 records (8 include, 92 maybe) and excluded 383. Agreement was 431 of 483 records (89.2 percent) across the three categories, Cohen kappa 0.62; for the binary pass against exclude split, agreement was 439 of 483 (90.9 percent), kappa 0.67. The kappa values sit in the substantial range on the Landis and Koch scale, and they are pulled down by prevalence: 382 of 483 records were excluded by both screeners, which raises expected agreement to 0.72 and lowers kappa at a given level of observed agreement. The binary kappa is the decision-relevant figure, because pass against exclude is the split that built the full-text list. All 52 disagreements were read by a screening adjudicator, who read the title, the abstract and both screeners reasons for each record and decided 9 passes to full text and 43 exclusions, with none flagged for the accountable author. The full-text list held 58 reports: 57 passed by both screeners and 1 added by adjudication.

Full-text screening applied a fixed hierarchy of exclusion reasons, coded FT-R1 to FT-R9, with the first applicable reason in the hierarchy recorded as the primary reason. Reports not retrieved and reports excluded are counted in the results.

Citation-chasing candidates were screened by two independent screeners under the same eligibility rules. One candidate, CC2-03, drew a final-decision disagreement and was settled by an independent adjudicator who read the full text and its supplement and included it.

### 3.6. Data extraction

Two extractors, working apart from each other and from the saved full texts only, extracted every included study onto a form specified before extraction (stage3-extraction/extraction-form.md). The form captured, per arm: citation, DOI, year, journal, country, specialty, summary type, source data (real or synthetic), model family and name, fine-tuning, retrieval and agentic flags, rater blinding, the number of summaries assessed, and the per-summary counts for each outcome, with derivation rules that allowed a count to be derived only from a printed percentage and a printed denominator whose product is a whole number. Each extractor produced 22 arm rows (21 LLM arms and 1 human comparator arm).

The two passes were compared field by field by code over 17 mapped fields across 22 arms: 374 field pairs, of which 161 differed as raw strings, a raw discrepancy rate of 43.0 percent. That headline rate needs its explanation beside it. Most raw differences were format artefacts: a blank cell against the literal code for not reported, parenthetical wording, and one pass carrying any-error values in mapping fields rather than in a dedicated column. Every numeric secondary count that both extractors reported as a number agreed exactly before settlement. Six substantive items went to a settlement reader, who re-read the source for each field and produced the settled sheet (stage3-extraction/extraction-settled.csv); the comparison file (extraction-comparison.csv) and the settlement log (settlement-log.md) are named here so the trail can be followed. Settlements changed pooled inputs in three places: the Park 2026 arms were re-keyed so that the critical-error count of 13 and the any-error count of 25 sit in the prospective pilot arm of 62 summaries; the Dhanda 2026 arms were re-keyed model by model with risk-fact omission counts of 13, 4, 5, 4, 6, 4, 2 and 4; and a derived total of 71 errors for Ganzinger 2025 was removed because it rested on multiplying a rounded mean, which the form rule does not allow. One item could not be fully resolved and is carried with its caveat: in the Park 2026 pilot, the text alternates between calling the 13 items errors and cases, and the Table 2 footnote warns that error counts do not sum to cases. The statistician worked from the settled sheet only.

### 3.7. Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed in duplicate with the adapted QUADAS-2 tool for LLM clinical summarisation evaluations (adapted from QUADAS-2 with QUADAS-AI signalling questions), specified in writing before extraction (stage3-extraction/rob-form.md) and named as adapted at every mention in this manuscript. The adapted tool keeps the four-domain structure of QUADAS-2 and re-aims it at generation evaluations: Domain 1, source-data selection; Domain 2, index generation process; Domain 3, reference standard and error adjudication; Domain 4, flow and timing. Each domain is rated low, some concerns, high or no information, with a support line from the paper for every rating. Judgements from the adapted tool are not merged with judgements from unmodified QUADAS-2, RoB 2 or ROBINS-I anywhere in this review.

Two assessors rated all 11 studies on all four domains, working apart. Both returned the same overall distribution: high 9, some concerns 2, low 0. Overall judgements disagreed on 0 of 11 studies. Domain cells disagreed on 12 of 44 (Domain 1: 5; Domain 2: 1; Domain 3: 2; Domain 4: 4). An adjudicator read the papers and both rationales for all 12 cells and settled 2 cells at the first assessor judgement and 10 at the second, none at a third value (stage3-extraction/rob-settled.csv). Two overall-rule applications were checked in full: two studies (R0167 and R0370) carried three or more domains at some concerns, and the adjudicator confirmed overall some concerns for both, because the form route from that pattern to high applies only where the combination leaves selection, generation and rating unchecked, which neither study meets.

### 3.8. Statistical analysis

Proportions were pooled with a random-effects generalised linear mixed model with a logit link, a binomial likelihood at unit level and a normal random effect on the logit scale, estimated by maximum likelihood. The unit of analysis was the study arm (a model, condition or summary set within a study), so a study could contribute more than one unit to a pool; unit and study counts are reported together for every pool for that reason. No continuity correction and no transformation to another scale were applied. Boundary counts (0 events, or events equal to the denominator) were retained; the binomial likelihood handles them.

For each pool the report gives the pooled proportion with its 95 percent confidence interval, tau squared (between-unit variance on the logit scale), I squared (the GLMM analogue computed as tau squared divided by tau squared plus the Higgins-Thompson typical within-unit sampling variance on the logit scale), and, for pools with at least 3 units, a 95 percent prediction interval computed on the logit scale and back-transformed. Unit-level intervals in the forest plots are Clopper-Pearson exact intervals. Single-unit outcomes carry exact (Clopper-Pearson) confidence intervals. Hartung-Knapp adjustment was specified in the analysis plan for the primary pool only; the primary pool does not exist, so no Hartung-Knapp fit was fittable and none is reported. No funnel plot test, Egger test or trim-and-fill estimate was run, per the analysis plan; for single-arm proportions with this level of heterogeneity, such tests would not separate reporting bias from genuine spread.

**Software disclosure.** The analysis plan named R with the metafor package as the primary route. That route could not be run in the build environment: R was not installed, the package installation route timed out, and the fallback statistics library could not be installed because the package proxy returned an HTTP 403. The identical GLMM-logit model was therefore fitted with a hand-written Python implementation (Python 3.12.3, numpy 1.26.4, scipy 1.11.4), integrating the random effect with 21-point adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature. The R entry file in the analysis folder is an unexecuted stub that documents this; the Python file is the code that produced every number. A first, non-adaptive quadrature implementation produced a wrong likelihood surface at large tau squared. It was caught by validation against per-unit numerical integration before any number was reported, and all of its outputs were discarded. The adaptive implementation agrees with the integration route to 6 decimal places on the headline pool.

**Checks.** The statistician hand-checked the supplementary any-error pool end to end (the pre-specified primary pool check was transferred to it, because the primary pool does not exist): an independent recomputation by a separate route (per-unit adaptive numerical integration with a different optimiser) gave a pooled proportion of 0.6055 (95 percent CI 0.2986 to 0.8470) with tau squared 1.9649, matching the analysis script to rounding. Crude totals (the summed numerators and denominators) were re-summed from the settled sheet for all 15 fitted pools and matched the analysis inputs exactly. Eight unit proportions were recomputed by hand, including the primary unit (13 of 62) and the outlying fabrication unit (654 of 671).

### 3.9. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroups were pre-specified in the protocol: summary type (discharge against non-discharge), source data (real against synthetic or benchmark records), rater blinding (blinded against unblinded or not reported), and model family. Subgroup analyses were run for the any-error and omission pools only, where unit counts allowed. A subgroup level with fewer than 2 units was not fitted; where any level had fewer than 2 units, or the pool had fewer than 5 units, results are descriptive only. Interaction tests, where run, were likelihood-ratio tests from a moderator GLMM with a common tau squared across levels. P values are unadjusted across the pre-specified families. Model family was tabulated descriptively only, because several family levels rest on a single study.

Sensitivity analyses were pre-specified: S1, restricted to studies with explicit severity or harm grading (applied to the secondary pools as the severity-graded analogue set in the analysis plan and Amendment 01); S2, excluding preprints; S3, excluding studies at high overall risk of bias on the adapted tool. One further sensitivity was required by the settled data and is reported beside the omission pool wherever it appears: exclusion of R0354, whose omission counts are specific to a single embedded risk fact per synthetic vignette rather than general omission audits.

### 3.10. Certainty of the evidence

Certainty was rated with GRADE, per outcome, by an assessor working from the settled extraction sheet, the settled risk of bias judgements and the settled analysis outputs, under a framework specified before results (stage4-analysis/grade-framework.md). Proportion evidence in this series starts at low certainty, because the body consists of observational evaluation studies without a comparator; the starting point is a property of the design set, and no domain is downgraded twice for the same concern. No upgrading factor was pre-committed and none was applied. Four pressure points were named in the framework before results: definition heterogeneity across studies (P1), the synthetic data share (P2), unblinded or unreported rating in Domain 3 of the adapted tool (P3), and inconsistency that pre-specified subgroups do not credibly explain (P4). Full domain-by-domain reasons for every rating are in stage4-analysis/grade-assessment.md, and the summary of findings table stands before the Background, as the series standard requires.

### 3.11. Deviations from the protocol

Recorded plainly: (1) Amendment 01 added the supplementary any-error pool, for the reasons in Section 3.3; (2) the statistics were fitted with the disclosed Python fallback in place of R and metafor (Section 3.8); (3) no separate extraction pilot phase was run (Section 3.3); (4) no separate forward citation-chasing pass was performed (Section 3.4); (5) OpenAlex was blocked and not searched, and seven licensed or specialist databases were not run (Section 3.4). No deviation changed an eligibility rule, an outcome definition or a pooled input after analysis began.

The worked example is Williams 2025 (R0231, with its preprint R0082). That study did the hard part of the work this review values: 100 encounters, LLM and physician narratives, blinded attending reviewers, errors counted and typed. It reported the result as means: 2.91 (SD 2.54) unique errors per LLM narrative against 1.82 (1.94) per physician narrative, with per-type means of 0.93 inaccuracies, 1.75 omissions and 0.23 hallucinations per LLM narrative. What it never reported is the number of narratives that contained at least one error. A mean cannot be turned back into that count: a mean of 2.91 is consistent with every narrative carrying errors and with a minority carrying many. The study was therefore excluded under the pre-specified F6 rule, in both its reports, and it could not enter the errors per summary pool either, because that outcome was specified for included studies. One extra column in its results table would have made it the largest study in this review and the source of the matched LLM against human contrast that Section 4.12 records as missing.

Two of the classes in Table 5 deserve a distinction. Vasilev 2025 (R0169) collected binary per-summary hallucination ratings and then published them only as converted mean scores, so the count existed and was lost at the reporting step. Singh 2026 (R0356) counted errors at field level by design, across 401 admissions, and its per-patient mean burden (2.484 omissions per patient in LLM summaries) answers a field-level question that a per-summary count would not answer better. The reporting gap is therefore not a single failure repeated 31 times. It is a set of reasonable measurement choices, made study by study, whose combined effect is that the per-summary risk of a clinically significant error, the quantity a service lead needs, is almost nowhere on the record.

### 4.6. Primary outcome: summaries with at least one clinically significant error (not pooled)

One unit in the entire included set reports this outcome: the prospective pilot arm of Park 2026 (R0301-2), a GPT-4o retrieval-augmented agent summarising breast surgical oncology clinic records, in which 13 of 62 summaries (21.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 11.7 to 33.2) carried an error the study classified as critical and treatment-impacting. No pooled estimate exists, and none is presented.

The single count carries a caveat that settlement could not remove. The paper alternates between calling the 13 items errors and cases, and the footnote to its Table 2 warns that error counts do not sum to cases with errors. The count is carried as printed, under the abstract survey-case framing. If some cases carried more than one critical error, the true number of summaries with at least one critical error would be lower than 13, and the text alone cannot settle the point. The pilot raters were unblinded, and the study is at high overall risk of bias on the adapted tool, with Domain 3 at high. GRADE certainty for this outcome is very low (Section 4.14).

One further study, Nakano 2026 (R0391), defined a major, clinically significant threshold but reported major counts at error level only (11 major instances), never as the number of summaries with at least one major error. It contributes no unit. The remaining 9 studies did not grade clinical significance per summary at all. The not pooled verdict is a result about the literature, and the discussion treats it as one.

### 4.9. Fabrication or addition

Eight units from 6 studies, covering 1226 summaries, gave a pooled proportion of 17.6 percent (95 percent CI 2.9 to 60.0), with tau squared 7.10, I squared 98.6 percent and a prediction interval of 0.1 to 98.3 percent. GRADE certainty is very low.

This pool carries a warning that the brief for the analysis stated and this manuscript repeats in full. One unit, Bodanki 2026 (R0429), an Epic-embedded GPT-4 system reported as a preprint, counts summaries with at least one unsupported statement at the level of atomic content units, and reports 654 of 671 summaries (97.5 percent) at or above that bar. Most other units sit below 25 percent: the range across units runs from 0.0 percent (Tung 2024, 0 of 5, a retained boundary count) to 97.5 percent. The crude total is 787 of 1226 (64.2 percent), dominated by the Bodanki unit, which contributes 671 of the 1226 summaries and 654 of the 787 events. The GLMM, weighting units toward equality, pulls the pooled estimate to 17.6 percent with an interval that spans most of the scale. With tau squared of 7.10 and a prediction interval of 0.1 to 98.3 percent, the single pooled figure is close to uninterpretable as a frequency. The unit-level figures in Figure 5 carry the information in this pool, and the pooled number should not be quoted without them.

### 4.10. Inaccuracy

Five units from 3 studies (Williams 2025, Jolin-Rodrigo 2026 and Grolleau 2026), covering 500 summaries, gave a pooled proportion of 27.9 percent (95 percent CI 14.6 to 46.7), with tau squared 0.81, I squared 93.4 percent and a prediction interval of 5.2 to 73.0 percent. The crude total was 153 of 500 (30.6 percent). This is a small pool, and its tau squared is imprecisely estimated. The one human arm in the dataset (Tung 2024 human arm, inaccuracy 0 of 10) enters no LLM pool and is excluded here by design. GRADE certainty is very low.

### 4.11. Harm at least moderate, and errors per summary

**Harm at least moderate (not pooled).** One unit reports this outcome per summary: Grolleau 2026 (R0438), Gemini 2.5 Pro hospital-course narratives, in which 1 of 100 summaries (1.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 0.03 to 5.4) was rated likely to cause moderate harm (AHRQ harm level 3 in the paper numbering). Levels 4 and 5 were explicitly zero in that study. Williams 2025 graded harm per error, not per summary, so no second unit exists. The estimate rests on a single event in a single study at high overall risk of bias, with unblinded rating. One rating decision changed would double the count or reduce it to zero. GRADE certainty is very low, and the low event count is not evidence that harm is rare across systems; it is one rated summary in one study.

**Errors per summary (not pooled).** Two units report per-summary error means on a comparable counting unit. Ganzinger 2025 (R0123) reports a mean of 2.84 errors per summary (SD 1.71, n 25). Nakano 2026 (R0391) reports 20 total errors over 11 summaries, a mean of 1.82, with a median of 2.0, an interquartile range of 1.0 to 2.0 and a range of 0 to 5, but no SD or standard error. Only one unit carries a dispersion measure, so no pooled mean can be computed without inventing a variance for the second. The outcome is reported narratively, as here. The Bodanki 2026 figure of 7.11 is excluded from this outcome in full: it is a mean per statement (4770 unsupported atomic content units out of 40,452 statements), a different counting unit from errors per summary, and pooling it with per-summary counts would mix two different quantities. GRADE certainty for the narrative body is very low.

### 4.12. LLM versus human contrast (not fittable)

No study reports matched LLM and human per-summary error counts for the same source records under the same outcome definition, so the contrast is not fittable and no cross-study comparison is presented as a contrast. The detail shows how close the literature comes without reaching the comparison. Tung 2024 reports LLM fabrication (0 of 5) but not human fabrication, and human inaccuracy (0 of 10) but not LLM inaccuracy, so no matched outcome pair exists in that study. In Ganzinger 2025 the physician summaries were used for similarity metrics and were not error rated. In Park 2026 the human summaries were the comparison reference and their errors were not counted. The single human arm in the dataset enters no pool. GRADE: not rateable, because there is no body of evidence to rate. The absence is reported as a gap, not as a finding of no difference.

### 4.13. Summary of sensitivity and subgroup findings

Across pools, the sensitivity analyses move the estimates without narrowing them. The any-error pool rises under S1 (72.5 percent) and under S3 (80.7 percent, a single study). The omission pool rises under every restriction (S1 41.9 percent, S2 34.2 percent, S3 55.0 percent, Dhanda exclusion 40.6 percent), each time because low-rate units leave the pool. The fabrication pool barely moves when its dominant crude contributor leaves (S2 9.7 percent against a main 17.6 percent, on a much lower crude base). The inaccuracy pool moves least (S2 20.3 percent, S3 30.1 percent against a main 27.9 percent). No sensitivity analysis produces a narrow interval, and none changes a GRADE rating. The subgroup record is in Sections 4.6 and 4.7: one null interaction (discharge, p = 0.734), and two hypothesis-generating interactions for omission (source data, p = 0.044; blinding, p = 0.032), each unadjusted and each confounded with study.

### 4.14. Certainty of the evidence

Every rateable outcome is rated very low certainty, and the LLM versus human contrast is not rateable. All proportion outcomes start at low under the GRADE approach for prevalence and proportion evidence used in this series. The downgrades that follow are named per outcome in the GRADE assessment (stage4-analysis/grade-assessment.md) and summarised here. Risk of bias pressed on every outcome except inaccuracy: 9 of 11 studies are at high overall risk on the adapted tool, rating was blinded in only 2 of 16 omission units, and the single-unit outcomes rest entirely on unblinded, high Domain 3 rating processes. Inconsistency pressed hardest: I squared is above 93 percent in every pool, tau squared reaches 7.10 in the fabrication pool, and prediction intervals span most of the proportion scale, with no subgroup split that credibly explains the spread. Indirectness pressed through definition heterogeneity (studies counting different events under the same error labels, and, in the omission pool, 8 of 16 units counting a single risk fact) and, for omission, through a synthetic share of 450 of 1011 summaries (44.5 percent). Imprecision pressed on the single-unit outcomes (62 summaries and 13 events for the primary; 1 event in 100 summaries for harm) and on the wide pooled intervals elsewhere. No upgrade was applied to any outcome.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1. Principal findings

This review set out to pool one number: the proportion of LLM-generated clinical summaries containing at least one clinically significant error. It could not. Of 11 included studies, 1 reported that outcome per summary (Park 2026, prospective pilot: 13 of 62 summaries, 21.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 11.7 to 33.2), and that single count is ambiguous in its own source text between errors and cases. Every other pooled figure in this review answers an easier question. The supplementary any-error pool (post hoc, Amendment 01) gave 60.6 percent (95 percent CI 29.8 to 84.7; 5 units, 4 studies, 323 summaries). Omission gave 20.7 percent (95 percent CI 11.5 to 34.6; 16 units, 7 studies, 1011 summaries), or 40.6 percent (95 percent CI 22.0 to 62.4) without the risk-fact-specific units. Fabrication or addition gave 17.6 percent (95 percent CI 2.9 to 60.0; 8 units, 6 studies, 1226 summaries), with a prediction interval of 0.1 to 98.3 percent that makes the single figure close to uninterpretable as a frequency. Inaccuracy gave 27.9 percent (95 percent CI 14.6 to 46.7; 5 units, 3 studies, 500 summaries). Harm of at least moderate level rests on 1 rated summary in 1 study (1 of 100, 1.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 0.03 to 5.4). No study reported a matched LLM versus human comparison. Certainty is very low for every rateable outcome.

### 5.2. Frequency and severity: the central tension

The studies in this review counted errors often and graded them rarely, and that imbalance is the central tension in the results. A count of any error, however small, is a frequency measure. In the supplementary pool it reaches 60.6 percent (95 percent CI 29.8 to 84.7), and in the severity-graded sensitivity of the same pool it reaches 72.5 percent (95 percent CI 48.7 to 88.0). A count of errors graded clinically significant is a severity measure, and the literature supplies exactly one per-summary version of it: 21.0 percent (95 percent CI 11.7 to 33.2), from 62 summaries in one pilot. A reader who takes the frequency figures as severity figures will overstate danger. A reader who takes the single severity figure as settled will overstate knowledge, because its interval runs from about 1 summary in 9 to about 1 summary in 3 and its numerator may count error instances rather than summaries. The harm outcome shows the same tension from the other side: 1 of 100 summaries rated likely to cause moderate harm (1.0 percent, exact 95 percent CI 0.03 to 5.4) is a small number built from a single rating decision, and it cannot carry a reassurance. Frequency is what the field measures. Severity is what services need. The distance between the two, in this literature, is the finding.

### 5.3. Why the primary outcome failed to pool is a finding

The primary outcome failed to pool for a plain reason: study reports do not carry the count. The full-text exclusion record shows the same pattern before inclusion is even reached: of the 48 reports assessed at full text, 31 (65 percent) reported no extractable per-summary error count and were excluded under FT-R7, and Section 4.5 classifies all 31 by what they reported instead. The discharge summary study by Williams and colleagues (JAMA Internal Medicine 2025) is the clearest case: a careful blinded comparison that reports mean errors per summary (2.91 LLM, 1.82 physician) and so can enter no proportion pool in this review. Inside the included set, Nakano 2026 defined a major threshold and then reported it at error level only (11 major instances across 11 summaries), one reporting step away from contributing the second primary unit this review needed. Park 2026 reported the count and left its unit ambiguous. A literature that evaluates safety-relevant systems without routinely reporting how many summaries contained a serious error has a reporting gap, and the gap is measurable: 1 of 11 studies. This review treats that count as a result, stated in the abstract and the summary of findings, rather than as a defect to be worked around with a quieter headline.

### 5.4. What the pooled figures can and cannot carry

Heterogeneity is very high in every pool: I squared 95.0 percent for any error, 94.6 percent for omission, 98.6 percent for fabrication or addition and 93.4 percent for inaccuracy, with prediction intervals of 6.9 to 97.0, 1.6 to 80.4, 0.1 to 98.3 and 5.2 to 73.0 percent respectively. A prediction interval that covers most of the scale says that a new study, in a new setting with a new system, could find almost any proportion. The pooled means are therefore summaries of a spread, and the spread is the information. The fabrication pool makes the point in its sharpest form: unit proportions run from 0.0 percent to 97.5 percent because the counting units differ, with one Epic-embedded system study (Bodanki 2026, extracted from its preprint) counting summaries with at least one unsupported atomic content unit at 654 of 671, while other studies counted summary-level hallucination or confabulation labels and sat below 25 percent. Pooling across counting units produces a number, 17.6 percent (95 percent CI 2.9 to 60.0), that describes no study in the pool well. The omission pool has the same structure in a milder form: 8 risk-fact-specific units at 2 to 13 events per 50 summaries pull the pooled estimate to 20.7 percent, and removing them gives 40.6 percent, so the honest report is the pair.

### 5.5. Risk of bias and the rating problem

Every numerator in this review is a judgement made by a rater against a source record, and the quality of that judgement process is where the evidence is weakest. On the adapted QUADAS-2 tool for LLM clinical summarisation evaluations (adapted from QUADAS-2 with QUADAS-AI signalling questions), 9 of 11 studies are at high overall risk of bias, 2 are at some concerns and none is at low risk, with Domain 3 (reference standard and error adjudication) at high for 7 studies and at no information for 1. Blinding is the sharpest gap: in the omission pool, 2 of 16 units were rated blind, 1 was unblinded and 13 do not report blinding. The blinding subgroup interaction for omission (blinded 61.7 percent against unblinded or not reported 16.8 percent, p = 0.032) rests on a single blinded study and is confounded with it, so it cannot show that blinding changes counts; it can only underline that the field rarely reports the safeguard at all. Sensitivity S3, which keeps only the 2 studies at some concerns, moves every pool it touches (any error 80.7 percent, omission 55.0 percent, fabrication 25.9 percent, inaccuracy 30.1 percent) without narrowing any interval, because 2 studies cannot carry a pool.

### 5.6. Relation to other work

This review differs from the registered discharge-summary review (PROSPERO CRD420261349028) in scope, and the difference shows in the data: the discharge subgroup in the omission pool holds 2 units and its interaction test is null (p = 0.734), while the non-discharge stratum, which the registered review excludes by design, supplies 14 of the 16 omission units and most of the other pools. The two reviews will therefore answer different questions even where their study sets overlap. Against the wider primary literature, the pattern in Section 5.3 holds: the three anchor studies a reader is most likely to expect here (Williams, JAMA Internal Medicine 2025; Van Veen 2024; Asgari 2025) were each excluded at full text because their reports do not yield a per-summary error numerator and denominator. That is a statement about reporting formats, not about the quality of those studies.

### 5.7. Strengths and limitations

Strengths, stated with their evidence: the protocol and every instrument (screening rules, extraction form, the adapted risk of bias tool, the GRADE framework, the analysis plan) were written before the stage they govern; screening, extraction and risk of bias were each done twice independently, with agreement measured (screening kappa 0.62 three-category and 0.67 binary) and every disagreement settled by reading; the extraction discrepancy rate (43.0 percent raw by field, mostly format artefacts, with all numeric outcome counts in agreement before settlement) is reported rather than hidden; the pooled any-error estimate was recomputed by an independent numerical route and matched to rounding; and the one amendment was logged, dated and labelled before analysis.

Limitations, stated plainly. OpenAlex was blocked by a provider rate limit and was not searched. Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, CENTRAL, IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library were not run, because no access route was available; ready strings are in the search log, and a review that runs them may find studies this one missed. Ten of 58 full texts sought were not retrieved, and any of them could hold an eligible study. No separate forward citation-chasing pass was performed. The primary outcome could not be pooled because the literature almost never reports a study-defined clinically significant count per summary, and the one included count (Park 2026) is ambiguous between errors and cases. The fabrication pool is dominated by one outlying unit. Heterogeneity is very high in every pool (I squared above 93 percent) and prediction intervals span most of the scale, so pooled figures are spread summaries, not system properties. Statistics were fitted with a hand-written Python GLMM (scipy, adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature) because R was unavailable in the build environment; the pooled any-error estimate was independently recomputed by a second numerical route and matched, and the analysis code is published with the review package. The review team was an LLM team with one accountable human author; that arrangement is disclosed in the declarations and carries the limits set out there.

## 6. Conclusions

### 6.1. Implications for practice

This section concerns how summarization systems should be evaluated, audited and reported. It makes no clinical recommendation and no medication recommendation, and nothing in it is advice for the care of an individual patient.

First, evaluation practice: a system should not be described as safe or unsafe for summary drafting on the strength of an any-error count alone. In this review the any-error figure (60.6 percent, 95 percent CI 29.8 to 84.7) and the one clinically significant figure (21.0 percent, 95 percent CI 11.7 to 33.2) measure different things, and a service that tracks only the first cannot see the second. Evaluations should grade errors for clinical significance at the time of rating, per summary, as a routine step.

Second, audit practice: audits should report the rating process with the counts. In the included studies, rating was blinded in a small minority of units (2 of 16 omission units), risk of bias on the adapted tool was high for 9 of 11 studies, and harm grading per summary appeared once (1 of 100 summaries at moderate level, with higher levels at zero). An audit that names its raters, its blinding, its error definitions and its harm scale can be compared with the next audit. An audit that reports a bare percentage cannot.

Third, reporting practice for vendors and study teams: publish, for each model version and summary type, the number of summaries assessed, the number with at least one error of each type, and the number with at least one clinically significant error, with the counting unit stated in words. The fabrication pool in this review (pooled 17.6 percent, prediction interval 0.1 to 98.3 percent) shows what happens to the evidence base when counting units differ in silence: the literature becomes impossible to combine, and no one can tell whether a system improved.

### 6.2. Implications for research

The first research need is the primary outcome itself. Studies should predefine a clinically significant error threshold, apply it per summary, and report the numerator and denominator. One study in 11 did so here, and a second (Nakano 2026) defined the threshold and stopped one step short. A small number of studies reporting that single count, across summary types, would let the next review pool the outcome this one could not.

The second need is the matched comparison. No included study counted errors in LLM and human summaries of the same records under the same definition, so the LLM versus human contrast is an empty cell in the summary of findings. A study that error-rates both drafts, blind to authorship, would fill the most decision-relevant gap in this literature.

The third need is counting discipline for fabrication. Studies should state whether fabrication is counted per summary, per statement or per content unit, and report the summary-level count even when a finer unit is the primary analysis. The Bodanki 2026 unit (654 of 671 summaries with at least one unsupported statement) and the summary-level units below 25 percent in the same pool cannot both inform a single frequency until that reporting step is standard.

The fourth need is replication across the gaps in this search: the databases that were not run, the 10 reports that were not retrieved, and forward citation chasing. A re-run of this search after the OpenAlex rate limit resets, and with licensed database access, is the cheapest available check on the completeness of the study set.
